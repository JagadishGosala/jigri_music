People tend to imagine folk music as performance. Most of it was not. It was
something you did while doing something else, and the shape of the song follows
the shape of the work.

## Songs built around work

The clearest example is the grinding song. A stone grinder turns at a steady
rate that a person cannot rush, and the songs that women sang over it are built
in long, even phrases with a repeated turn at the end of each line. The tune is
doing a job: it keeps the hands at a constant speed and makes an hour of dull
work pass.

Transplanting songs behave differently. That work is done bent over in a line of
people, and the songs are call-and-response — one voice throws a line down the
row and the rest answer it. You can hear the field in the structure.

## Songs built around ceremony

Wedding songs are the opposite. They are fixed, they are long, and they are
sung by people who are sitting still. Because nobody is working, the melodies
can be more elaborate and the verses can run to twenty or thirty lines without
losing anyone.

This is also the repertoire with the most local variation. A wedding song from
one district will share a first verse with the version from the next district
and then diverge completely.

## Why recording them changes them

The honest difficulty with a project like this one is that a recording freezes a
song that was never meant to be frozen. The version we publish becomes *the*
version for anyone who finds it here.

We try to handle that by saying where each song came from and who we learned it
from, rather than presenting it as definitive. If you know a different version
of something we have posted, we would genuinely like to hear it.
